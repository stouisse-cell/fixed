Visibility, Information and Time: The Three Gaps in Morocco’s Property Market

Morocco does not lack good properties, and it does not lack serious buyers. What it often lacks is the connection between them. Three gaps explain why that connection fails, and understanding them is the first step towards buying with confidence.

Published
1 November 2026
By
Rahim International
Read Time
9 minutes

Anyone who has tried to buy property in Morocco, whether from Kénitra, from Casablanca or from another continent, knows that the difficulty is rarely a lack of choice. New developments are being built in almost every growing city, resale apartments come onto the market every week, and many projects offer real value. Yet buyers regularly tell us the same thing: they searched for months, visited many properties, and still did not feel confident enough to decide. At the same time, developers of well-built projects tell us that their apartments sell more slowly than they should. When good supply and serious demand exist side by side but fail to meet, the problem is not the market itself. It is the distance between the two sides. In our experience, that distance has three components: visibility, information and time.

The problem is rarely a lack of opportunities. It is the distance, the missing information and the lack of time that separate good projects from the buyers who would value them.

The Visibility Gap: Good Projects That Buyers Never See

A large part of the Moroccan new-build market is still sold in a traditional way. A developer builds a sales office on the site, places a sign at the entrance and relies on people who pass by, on word of mouth and on a small number of local intermediaries. Many projects, particularly during the construction stage, invest very little in digital marketing. Plans, prices and specifications are rarely published online, and when they are, the information is often incomplete or out of date. For a buyer who lives in the same neighbourhood, this is not a serious obstacle. For a buyer who lives in another city, or in France, Canada or Belgium, the project effectively does not exist.

This has a consequence that many buyers do not realise. The properties that are most visible online are not necessarily the best ones. They are simply the ones whose sellers or agents have invested the most in being seen. A well-built development with a strong location and a competitive price can remain almost invisible, while weaker projects with larger advertising budgets reach far more buyers. A buyer who searches only through listing websites and social media therefore sees a partial picture of the market, and may compare five options when the best one was never on the list.

The visibility gap affects developers as well. A serious developer who builds carefully and prices fairly has every interest in reaching informed buyers, because those buyers are the ones most likely to recognise the quality of the work. When the project is only presented through a sales office, it competes on the same level as projects that are less carefully built, and the difference in quality is not communicated to the people who would value it most.

The Information Gap: Buying Without a Point of Reference

The second gap concerns information. In many markets, a buyer can consult recent transaction prices, compare similar properties and form a reasonable idea of what a fair price is before visiting anything. In Morocco, there is no simple and reliable public source of recent transaction prices that a buyer can consult before making an offer. Asking prices vary widely, listings often omit important details such as the exact surface, the floor, the orientation or the legal status of the property, and the same apartment can be offered at different prices depending on who is asking. Buyers who live abroad are sometimes quoted higher prices than local buyers, simply because the seller assumes they do not know the market.

The information gap is not only about price. It also concerns the quality of what is being sold. Poor construction is not always visible during a short visit, particularly in a new building where finishes can hide weaknesses in insulation, waterproofing or materials. Some developers have a long record of delivering on time and to specification, while others have a history of delays, changes to plans or unfinished common areas. For an off-plan purchase, the reliability of the developer is often more important than the apartment itself, because the buyer is committing to a building that does not yet exist. Without local knowledge, it is difficult to tell these situations apart.

Before committing to any property, a buyer should be able to answer four questions: what similar properties in the same area have recently sold for, what the developer or seller has delivered in the past, whether the legal and administrative situation of the property is clear, and why this property is better than the alternatives at the same budget.

When these questions cannot be answered, buyers tend to rely on simple rules that feel safe but often are not. A larger apartment seems to offer more value than a smaller one, a finished building seems safer than an off-plan project, and a lower price per square metre seems like a better deal. Each of these rules can be true, but each can also be misleading. A larger apartment in a weaker location, or built to a lower standard, can be a worse investment than a smaller one in a better building. The only reliable answer comes from comparing real options with real information.

The Time Gap: The Most Important of the Three

The third gap is time, and in our experience it is the most important, because it is the one that makes the other two so difficult to overcome. Buying property is one of the largest financial decisions most people make in their lifetime. Making that decision well requires looking at many options, comparing them, researching prices and developers, visiting several times at different hours of the day, and inspecting carefully before committing. Most people take several weeks to choose a sofa or a refrigerator. It would be reasonable to expect a property purchase to take considerably longer.

The traditional Moroccan souk illustrates this well. Few people buy from the first stall they visit. They walk through the market, compare two or three stalls, ask the prices, look at the quality and negotiate. Only once they understand what a fair price is and what good quality looks like do they return, very often to the first stall, and buy with confidence. The property market works in the same way, except that the stalls are spread across a whole city, the prices are not displayed, and each visit can take half a day.

This is exactly the time that most buyers do not have. A Moroccan living abroad typically has a few weeks of holiday in Morocco each year, and those weeks are also used for family, rest and other obligations. A resident who works full-time in Morocco has evenings and weekends at most. As a result, many buyers try to compress a search that would normally take months into a few days. Under that pressure, decisions are made at the end of a trip rather than at the end of a proper comparison. Buyers choose the property that looks safest at that moment, or they leave without deciding and start again the following year, often facing higher prices.

How the Three Gaps Reinforce Each Other

Each gap would be manageable on its own. A buyer with enough time could compensate for the lack of public information by visiting and comparing extensively. A buyer with good information could make a quick decision with confidence. A well-marketed project would reach buyers even if they had little time. The difficulty is that the three gaps usually occur together. The best projects are not easy to find, the information needed to evaluate them is not easy to obtain, and the buyer does not have the time to do either properly.

We saw this clearly in the experience that led to the creation of Rahim International. A relative of our founder, living in Canada, wanted to invest in an apartment in Kénitra. An off-plan project with a strong location, sound construction and a competitive price had already been identified, but it had almost no presence online and did not look convincing from abroad. She continued her search alone, through weeks of research from Canada and a trip filled with visits, without a reliable point of reference for prices or quality. As her departure date approached, she came close to buying a larger apartment that had weaknesses in construction and location that were not visible during a short visit. Once the market was analysed properly, the project she had first set aside proved to be the strongest option available. The opportunity had been there from the beginning. What was missing was visibility, information and time.

What Buyers Can Do About Each Gap

To reduce the visibility gap, buyers should not rely only on listing websites and social media. It is worth visiting the sales offices of developments under construction in the areas that interest you, asking local professionals which projects have a good reputation, and looking at the areas where new buildings are going up rather than only at the properties that are advertised. The best opportunities are often found at the construction stage, before the project becomes widely known.

To reduce the information gap, buyers should ask for prices in writing, compare at least three similar properties in the same area, visit buildings that the developer has already delivered, and speak to people who live in them if possible. For an off-plan purchase, it is essential to understand the payment schedule, the planned delivery date and the guarantees provided, and to have the documents reviewed before signing. A price should always be compared with what similar properties have actually sold for, not only with other asking prices.

To reduce the time gap, the most effective step is to start early and to separate the search from the decision. A buyer who defines clear objectives, prepares financing in advance and begins comparing options several months before a planned trip will arrive with a short list of serious candidates rather than an open search. It also helps to avoid making a final decision during the last days of a trip, when the pressure to conclude is strongest. When none of this is possible, delegating the research, the visits and the analysis to someone who is on the ground and who works in the buyer’s interest is often the most reliable solution.
